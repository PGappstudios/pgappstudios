*Educational content, not medical advice. Your midwife, health visitor, nurse or doctor knows your baby; please take any worry about growth to them.*

Few numbers cause as much late-night worry as a baby's percentile. One check-up says the 50th, the next says the 25th, and suddenly you are searching at 3 a.m. The good news is that percentiles are much less dramatic than they sound once you know what they measure.

## What a percentile actually means

A growth percentile compares your baby with a large group of healthy babies of the **same age and sex**. If your baby is on the **25th percentile for weight**, it means that about 25 out of every 100 healthy babies of that age weigh less, and about 75 weigh more.

It is not a score. The 90th percentile is not "better" than the 10th. Healthy babies sit everywhere on the chart, because healthy babies come in different sizes, often much like their parents.

## Which chart your baby is measured on

Most countries now use the **WHO Child Growth Standards**, published by the World Health Organization in 2006. They were built from healthy, breastfed babies raised in good conditions in six countries (Brazil, Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the United States), so they describe how babies grow when things go well, not just how an average population happened to grow.

- **United States:** the CDC recommends the WHO charts for babies under 2, and its own CDC charts from age 2.
- **United Kingdom:** the UK-WHO charts in your baby's Red Book (the Personal Child Health Record) use the WHO standards from two weeks to age four.
- **Australia:** the WHO charts are widely recommended for children under 2.

## The three measurements

Most checks track three things, each with its own chart:

1. **Weight for age.** The one everyone watches, and the one that moves around most.
2. **Length (or height) for age.** Measured lying down until about age two.
3. **Head circumference for age.** Measured around the widest part of the head, and tracked closely in the first year.

Percentiles across the three don't have to match. A long, lean baby might be on the 75th for length and the 25th for weight, and be perfectly healthy.

## Why the trend matters more than the number

Doctors care far more about **the shape of the line** than any single dot. A baby who follows the 9th percentile steadily is usually doing well. A baby who drops from the 75th to the 25th over a few months may need a closer look, even though the 25th is a normal place to be.

That is why one measurement on its own rarely means much. Scales differ, a feed or a nappy can change a weight reading, and babies grow in spurts. What matters is several measurements over time.

Some patterns your health professional may want to look at more closely:

- Weight **falling across two or more major percentile lines**.
- A baby who **hasn't regained their birth weight by about two weeks**. Losing some weight in the first days is normal.
- Measurements that are **very far from the rest of the chart** at either end.
- Growth worries **together with** feeding problems, fewer wet nappies, unusual sleepiness or other symptoms.

## The first two weeks are different

Almost all newborns lose some weight in the first few days while feeding gets going, and most are back to their birth weight by around two weeks. That is why the UK charts start from two weeks: the first days are expected to be bumpy.

## Babies born early: corrected age

If your baby was born preterm, their growth is usually plotted by **corrected age**, which is their age counted from their due date rather than their birth date. A baby born eight weeks early who is now four months old is plotted as about two months. Corrected age is typically used until around age two.

## How to talk about it at check-ups

- Ask **"is the line following a steady curve?"** rather than "is the number OK?"
- Bring your own record of past measurements if you have one.
- Mention feeding, nappies and sleep alongside the numbers. They are part of the picture.
- If something worries you between checks, call. You never need a percentile to justify asking.

## FAQ

### What is a normal percentile for a baby?

Any percentile can be normal. Healthy babies sit anywhere from the low to the high end of the chart. What matters is that your baby grows steadily along their own curve.

### Is the 50th percentile the ideal?

No. The 50th is simply the middle. A baby on the 10th or the 90th can be just as healthy, often reflecting their parents' size.

### Should I worry if my baby drops a percentile line?

A small move is common, especially between scales or around growth spurts. Weight falling across two or more major lines is something your health professional may want to check, so mention it at your next appointment or call sooner if you are worried.

### What growth chart does my doctor use?

Most doctors use the WHO Child Growth Standards for babies under two. In the US the CDC charts take over at age two; in the UK the UK-WHO charts in the Red Book run to age four.

### When should I use corrected age?

For babies born preterm, growth is usually plotted by corrected age (counted from the due date) until about two years old. Your baby's care team will tell you how they plot it.
