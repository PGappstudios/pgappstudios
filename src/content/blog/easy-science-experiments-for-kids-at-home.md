Children don't remember the worksheet. They remember the day the water climbed up a paper towel by itself, or a secret message appeared out of nowhere. Kitchen science is the cheapest, most memorable way to show a child that the world follows rules they can discover.

Every experiment below uses things most homes already have. Each one comes with the science in plain words, so you can answer the inevitable "but *why*?".

**Before you start:** an adult should be in charge of anything hot, sharp or breakable, and nothing here is for eating unless it says so. Wipe up spills as you go, and wash hands afterwards.

## 1. Rainbow density tower

**You need:** honey, washing-up liquid, water with food colouring, vegetable oil, a tall clear glass.

Pour each liquid slowly down the side of the glass, starting with the honey, then the washing-up liquid, then the coloured water, then the oil. They stack in layers instead of mixing.

**The science:** each liquid has a different **density**, meaning how much "stuff" is packed into the same space. The heaviest for its size sinks to the bottom and the lightest floats on top.

## 2. Secret lemon messages

**You need:** lemon juice, a cotton bud or paintbrush, white paper, and an adult with a hair dryer or a warm iron.

Write a message in lemon juice and let it dry until it disappears. An adult then gently heats the paper and the writing slowly turns brown and appears.

**The science:** heat makes the lemon juice react and brown faster than the paper around it, a bit like toast. Never use a candle or open flame for this one.

## 3. Bend water with a comb

**You need:** a plastic comb, dry hair (or a wool jumper), a tap.

Run a thin, steady stream of water. Comb dry hair several times, then hold the comb close to the stream without touching it. The water bends towards the comb.

**The science:** combing moves tiny electric charges onto the comb (**static electricity**). Water molecules have slightly charged ends, so they are pulled towards it.

## 4. The drinking celery

**You need:** a celery stick with leaves, a glass of water, food colouring.

Put the celery in strongly coloured water and leave it overnight. The next day the leaves are tinged with colour, and a cut stalk shows coloured dots.

**The science:** plants pull water up through thin tubes in their stems, a process helped by **capillary action** and by water evaporating from the leaves.

## 5. Walking water

**You need:** three or more glasses, kitchen paper, food colouring.

Fill every other glass with coloured water (say red and blue) and leave the ones between empty. Fold strips of kitchen paper to bridge each glass to the next. Over an hour or two, the water "walks" into the empty glasses and the colours mix.

**The science:** water climbs through the tiny gaps between paper fibres, again by **capillary action**, until the levels even out.

## 6. Baking soda volcano

**You need:** bicarbonate of soda (baking soda), vinegar, washing-up liquid, food colouring, a cup on a tray.

Put two spoonfuls of bicarbonate of soda in the cup with a squirt of washing-up liquid and a drop of colour. Pour in vinegar and watch it erupt.

**The science:** an acid (vinegar) and a base (bicarbonate of soda) react and release **carbon dioxide** gas. The soap traps the gas in bubbles, making the foam.

## 7. Dancing raisins

**You need:** a clear glass of sparkling water, a handful of raisins.

Drop the raisins in. They sink, then rise, then sink again, over and over.

**The science:** bubbles of carbon dioxide cling to the wrinkly raisins and lift them like tiny life jackets. At the surface the bubbles pop and the raisins sink again.

## 8. The floating egg

**You need:** two glasses of water, salt, two raw eggs.

An egg sinks in plain water. Stir lots of salt into the second glass and the egg floats.

**The science:** salt makes the water **denser**, so it pushes up harder on the egg. It is the same reason people float more easily in the sea than in a pool.

## 9. Milk colour explosion

**You need:** a shallow plate of full-fat milk, food colouring, a cotton bud, washing-up liquid.

Drip a few colours onto the milk. Touch the centre with a cotton bud dipped in washing-up liquid. The colours burst outwards and swirl.

**The science:** soap breaks the **surface tension** of the milk and grabs onto its fat, sending the colours racing away.

## 10. Rain cloud in a jar

**You need:** a jar of water, shaving foam, food colouring, a dropper or spoon.

Spray a "cloud" of shaving foam on top of the water. Drip coloured water onto the cloud. When it is too heavy, colour "rains" down into the jar.

**The science:** real clouds hold water droplets until they get too heavy, then it falls as rain. The foam shows that saturation point in slow motion.

## 11. Pepper and soap

**You need:** a bowl of water, ground pepper, washing-up liquid.

Sprinkle pepper over the water. Touch the middle with a soapy finger and the pepper shoots to the edges.

**The science:** water has a stretchy "skin" called **surface tension**. Soap weakens it where you touch, and the stronger pull at the edges drags the pepper away.

## 12. Make a rainbow

**You need:** a glass of water, a sunny window, white paper.

Put the glass on a sunny windowsill and hold the paper below it. Move it around until a small rainbow appears.

**The science:** sunlight is a mix of colours. Water bends each colour by a slightly different amount (**refraction**), spreading them out into a rainbow.

## Make it stick

- **Ask for a guess first.** "What do you think will happen?" turns a trick into a real experiment.
- **Change one thing.** Warm water instead of cold, more salt, a different liquid. That is how scientists work.
- **Draw it afterwards.** A quick sketch with a label or two helps children remember what they saw.

## FAQ

### What is the easiest science experiment for kids?

Dancing raisins and the floating egg need almost no setup and work every time, which makes them perfect for younger children.

### What age are these experiments for?

Most work from about age 4 with an adult helping, and older children can run them on their own and try changing one variable. An adult should always handle heat.

### Which experiments are safe without heat?

All except the secret lemon messages, which need an adult with a hair dryer or warm iron. Never use a candle for it.

### How do I explain the science to a young child?

Keep it to one idea: heavy things sink, water climbs through tiny gaps, soap breaks water's skin. Then let them test it again.
