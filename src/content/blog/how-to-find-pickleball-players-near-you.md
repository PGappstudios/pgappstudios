You bought a paddle. You're hooked after two sessions. And now you've hit the wall every new pickleball player hits: who do I actually play with? The sport is exploding, but that doesn't mean the players are easy to find, especially if you don't already know a crew. Here's how to fix that, from the old-school ways to the fast ones.

## Start at the courts

It sounds obvious, but the single best move is to physically show up at a court during busy hours. Pickleball culture is famously welcoming, and most public courts run a simple "paddle stack" system: you put your paddle in the rack or line it up, and you rotate into the next available game.

Go early evening on a weekday or mid-morning on a weekend and you'll usually find a rotation going. Say hi, ask if you can get in the next round, and you're playing within minutes. Nobody expects you to be good. They expect you to be friendly and to keep the paddles moving.

**Tip:** bring your own paddle but don't stress about gear. Ask which courts locals prefer — there's almost always one that's the unofficial hub.

## Join a local club

Clubs are the backbone of the sport. Many tennis clubs, community centres and recreation departments now run dedicated pickleball sessions, beginner clinics and organised open play. A membership or a small drop-in fee gets you a reliable schedule and a built-in group of regulars.

The bonus with clubs is structure. Beginner nights mean you're playing with people at your level instead of getting bulldozed by someone with a wicked third-shot drop. Clinics teach you the basics fast, and the people you meet there become your future playing partners.

## Facebook groups and local pages

Almost every town with a pickleball scene has a Facebook group. Search your city name plus "pickleball" and you'll usually find one. These groups post open-play times, organise casual meetups, and let you shout "anyone free Saturday morning?" into a crowd that actually answers.

The downside is that group chats get messy fast. Times change, threads bury important info, and it can be hard to know who's actually showing up. Still, it's a solid free way to plug into the local pulse and find out where people play.

## Community boards and word of mouth

Don't underestimate the low-tech route. Rec centres, gyms and even coffee shops near courts often have notice boards. Ask around. Pickleball players love recruiting new people — mention you're learning and someone will invite you along. The sport spreads person to person more than any marketing ever could.

## How to break in as a beginner

Finding players is one thing. Feeling comfortable showing up is another. A few things make it painless:

### Lead with honesty
Tell people you're new. Every decent player has been there, and most will happily slow down, feed you easy balls, and share a tip or two. Pretending you're better than you are is the only way to make it awkward.

### Learn the basic etiquette
Know how paddle-stacking rotation works, don't wander behind courts during a point, and call your own faults. Getting these small courtesies right earns you goodwill instantly.

### Go regularly
The magic happens with repetition. Show up to the same session three weeks running and suddenly you have names, a group chat, and standing invites. Consistency turns strangers into your pickleball family faster than anything.

### Say yes to everything at first
Different skill levels, different formats, morning or evening — take it all early on. You'll learn faster, meet more people, and quickly figure out which games and which crew fit you best.

## Put it together

The players are out there — the sport has never had more of them. Your job is just to get in front of them. Show up at the courts, join a club, plug into a local group, and use an app to cut out the guesswork. Do two or three of those in the same week and you'll go from "I have nobody to play with" to "I can't fit all these games in" faster than you'd think.

Now grab your paddle and go find your next game.
