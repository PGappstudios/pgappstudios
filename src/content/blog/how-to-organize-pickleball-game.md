Every pickleball crew has lived through it: the endless group chat where forty messages get sent, three people flake, nobody's sure which court, and somehow you end up with five players for four spots. Organising a game shouldn't be harder than playing one. Here's how to set up doubles or open play cleanly, keep it fair, and skip the chaos entirely.

## Step 1: Decide the format first

Before anything else, know what you're organising. The two most common casual setups are:

- **Fixed doubles:** four players, one game, you all play together for the session. Simple, social, great for a regular crew.
- **Open play / rotation:** more than four players sharing courts, rotating in and out using a paddle stack. Better for bigger groups and mixing partners.

Pick the format up front, because it changes how many people you need and how you'll run the session. Trying to decide on the fly is where confusion starts.

## Step 2: Choose the court and time

Lock these down early, because "where and when" is what people actually need to commit.

**Court:** Consider whether you need to book. Some courts are first-come public spaces where you queue; others (clubs, indoor centres) require a reservation. If it's a busy public court, have a backup in mind — nothing kills momentum like turning up to find every court packed.

**Time:** Be specific and realistic. "Sometime Saturday" gets you nowhere. "Saturday 9 to 11am at the community courts" gets commitments. Early mornings and weekday evenings are usually the sweet spots — cooler, less crowded, and they fit around work. Once you name an exact slot, people can give you a real yes or no.

## Step 3: Get firm commitments (this is the hard part)

The eternal problem is the "maybe." Maybes don't fill courts. When you organise, ask directly for a yes or no and set a cutoff — "let me know by Friday night." Aim for the exact number your format needs, plus one reliable backup in case someone drops.

For fixed doubles you need four. For open play, more is fine, but know your court count: two courts comfortably handle eight to twelve players in rotation. More than that and people spend too long sitting out, which is where a session loses its energy.

## Step 4: Balance the skill levels

This is what separates a fun session from a frustrating one. A game where one pair is miles stronger than the other isn't fun for anyone — the weaker players feel bulldozed and the stronger ones get bored.

A few ways to keep it balanced:

- **Match similar levels together** for competitive, close games.
- **Or deliberately split strong and developing players** across teams to even things out, which works great in casual mixed groups.
- **In open play, rotate partners** so the same mismatch doesn't repeat every game.

The key is knowing everyone's rough level before you start. If you're organising for people you don't know well, ask them to be honest about where they're at. Balanced games are the single biggest factor in whether people come back next week.

## Step 5: Confirm and remind

The day before, send one clear confirmation: who's in, exact court, exact time, and any details (bring balls, gate code, parking). Then let it be. A single clean reminder beats a hundred scattered messages. If someone drops, tap your backup. Done.

## The problem with doing all this by hand

Notice how much of the above is admin. Chasing yes-or-no answers, tracking who's in, remembering everyone's skill level, sending reminders, finding a replacement when someone flakes — it's a part-time job, and it usually falls on one poor organiser every single week. That's why so many would-be games die in the group chat before anyone picks up a paddle.
