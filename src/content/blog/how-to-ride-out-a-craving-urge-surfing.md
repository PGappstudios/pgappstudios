Every craving feels like it will last forever and keep getting stronger until you give in. It won't. Cravings behave like waves: they build, peak and then fall away, usually within minutes. Learning to ride the wave instead of fighting it is one of the most useful skills in any recovery.

## Why cravings happen

When a habit has been rewarding for a long time, your brain learns to expect it. Certain places, times, feelings and people become **triggers**: payday, a stressful call, the end of a long day, a friend offering you one. When a trigger hits, your brain sends a strong signal to repeat the habit.

The craving is that signal. It is uncomfortable, but it is not an order, and it is not a sign you are failing. People in long, stable recovery still get cravings. They have just learned what to do with them.

## Urge surfing, step by step

**Urge surfing** comes from mindfulness-based relapse prevention, developed by psychologist G. Alan Marlatt and colleagues. Instead of fighting the craving, you watch it rise and fall without acting on it.

1. **Notice and name it.** "This is a craving. It's a wave."
2. **Find it in your body.** Where do you feel it: chest, stomach, hands, jaw? Is it hot, tight, buzzing?
3. **Breathe into it.** Slow breaths, paying attention to the sensation rather than the story your mind tells about it.
4. **Watch it change.** Notice it getting stronger, peaking, then starting to fade. Rate it from 1 to 10 every minute if that helps.
5. **Let it pass.** When it drops, notice that you rode it out. That memory makes the next one easier.

The first few times feel awkward. That's normal. Like any skill, it gets easier with practice.

## Four more tools for the moment it hits

### 1. Breathe 4-7-8

Breathe in through your nose for **4** seconds, hold for **7**, and breathe out slowly through your mouth for **8**. Repeat four times. Slow, long exhales calm your body's stress response and give the wave time to pass.

### 2. Check HALT

Ask yourself whether you are **H**ungry, **A**ngry, **L**onely or **T**ired. Cravings are much stronger when one of those is true, and each has a simpler fix: eat something, step away, message someone, rest.

### 3. Play the tape forward

Don't stop the movie at the first drink, bet or cigarette. Run it forward: tonight, tomorrow morning, next week. What actually happens? Most people find the end of the tape is enough to change their mind.

### 4. Change something, fast

Leave the room, go for a walk, take a shower, call someone, drink a large glass of water. Changing your surroundings breaks the link between the trigger and the habit.

## Plan for the moments that break people

Cravings are predictable. Write a short plan for your hardest situations before they happen:

- **Late at night:** what you'll do instead, and who you can message.
- **Someone offers you one:** the exact words you'll say.
- **Payday or a windfall:** where the money goes first.
- **Anger or bad news:** your first two steps before anything else.
- **Missing it:** a reminder of why you stopped, in your own words.

## If you slip

A slip is information, not the end. What was the trigger? What was missing (food, sleep, support, a plan)? What will you do differently next time? Then start again. Shame is one of the biggest reasons people abandon recovery, and it doesn't help anyone stay stopped.

## FAQ

### How long does a craving last?

Most cravings peak and fade within minutes, even though they feel endless at the time. Riding them out without acting gets easier each time.

### What is urge surfing?

A mindfulness technique from relapse-prevention research: you notice a craving, feel where it sits in your body, and watch it rise and fall like a wave without acting on it.

### What should I do when a craving hits?

Name it, breathe slowly (try 4-7-8), check whether you are hungry, angry, lonely or tired, play the tape forward and change your surroundings. Then let it pass.

### Is it safe to stop drinking suddenly?

If you drink heavily every day, stopping suddenly can be dangerous. Talk to a doctor first so they can help you stop safely.

### Does relapsing mean I've failed?

No. A slip is information about what you need. Work out the trigger, adjust your plan and start again.
