If you are learning Spanish for a trip to Madrid, a move to Málaga or a summer in Barcelona, you have probably noticed that most courses quietly teach Latin American Spanish. That is not wrong. It is just not what you will hear in Spain.

The good news first: it is one language. A Mexican and a Spaniard understand each other perfectly well, much like an American and a Brit. The differences are real, though, and once you know them you will stop being surprised by them.

## 1. Vosotros: the pronoun Latin America doesn't use

The biggest grammar difference is how you say "you" to a group of people.

- **In Spain**, *vosotros* is the informal plural "you" (friends, family, kids), and *ustedes* is the formal one.
- **In Latin America**, *ustedes* covers everyone, formal and informal.

So in Spain you will hear *¿Qué queréis tomar?* ("What do you all want?") among friends, where most of Latin America says *¿Qué quieren tomar?*. That means one extra set of verb endings to learn for Spain, but they are regular and you pick them up fast.

## 2. The "th" sound

In most of Spain, the letter **z**, and **c** before **e** or **i**, is pronounced like the English **th** in *think*. *Gracias* sounds like "GRAH-thyas" and *cerveza* like "ther-BEH-tha".

This is called **distinción**, and it is not a lisp: an **s** is still an **s**. In Latin America (and in the Canary Islands and parts of Andalusia), those letters are simply pronounced **s**. People everywhere understand both.

Spaniards also tend to pronounce the **j** (as in *jamón*) with a stronger, throatier sound.

## 3. Everyday vocabulary

Most words are shared, but a handful of very common ones change. A few you will meet in your first week in Spain:

| English | Spain | Much of Latin America |
|---|---|---|
| car | coche | carro, auto |
| computer | ordenador | computadora |
| mobile phone | móvil | celular |
| juice | zumo | jugo |
| potato | patata | papa |
| glasses | gafas | lentes, anteojos |
| to drive | conducir | manejar |
| flat / apartment | piso | departamento, apartamento |
| OK | vale | (varies by country) |

You will hear *vale* constantly in Spain. It means OK, sure, got it, and fine, all at once.

## 4. "I have eaten" vs "I ate"

For things that happened today, Spain often uses the present perfect: *Hoy **he comido** paella* ("Today I have eaten paella"). Most of Latin America uses the simple past: *Hoy **comí** paella*.

Both are correct and both are understood. Using the Spain version simply makes you sound like you learned in Spain.

## 5. Formality and tone

Spaniards tend to switch to the informal *tú* quickly, even with strangers of a similar age, and speech can sound direct to outsiders. In many Latin American countries, *usted* is used more widely and politeness formulas run longer. Neither is ruder; they are just different habits.

## So which Spanish should you learn?

Learn the Spanish of the place you will actually spend time in:

- **Going to Spain, living there or dating someone from Spain?** Learn Castilian Spanish. You will understand people faster and sound more natural.
- **Travelling in Mexico, Colombia or Argentina, or working with Latin American colleagues?** Learn Latin American Spanish, ideally the variety of that country.
- **Not sure yet?** Pick one and stay consistent. Switching back and forth is what actually slows you down, and anything you learn transfers to the other.

## FAQ

### Can people from Spain and Latin America understand each other?

Yes, easily. The differences are like those between British and American English: accent, some vocabulary and a few grammar habits.

### Is Castilian Spanish harder to learn?

Only slightly. You learn one extra set of verb endings for *vosotros* and the "th" sound. Everything else is shared.

### Is the Spanish "th" sound a lisp?

No. It is a standard feature called *distinción*: z, and c before e or i, sound like "th", while s still sounds like s.

### Should I learn Spain Spanish or Latin American Spanish?

Learn the variety of the place you will spend the most time in. If that is Spain, learn Castilian; you will understand people faster.
