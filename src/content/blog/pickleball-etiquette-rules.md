Pickleball has a rulebook, but it also has a whole second layer of rules nobody writes down — the etiquette that separates the players everyone wants in their game from the ones people quietly avoid. None of it is complicated. Most of it is just being a decent human with a paddle. Here are the ten that matter most.

## 1. Call lines honestly — and generously

You call the lines on your side of the court. Nobody's refereeing, so the whole thing runs on trust. The golden rule: if you're not sure whether a ball was in or out, it's in. Give the benefit of the doubt to your opponent, always. Players who call every close ball their way get a reputation fast, and it's not a good one. Being fair — even generous — earns instant respect.

## 2. Tap paddles after every game

Win or lose, close or blowout, you tap paddles at the net (or the handle end, the classic pickleball fist-bump equivalent) with all three other players and say "good game." It's the universal sign-off. Skipping it reads as sulking. It takes two seconds and it keeps the whole vibe friendly.

## 3. Respect the open-play rotation

Open play runs on the paddle stack: you put your paddle in the rack or queue when you arrive, and you play when your paddle comes up. Don't jump the line, don't pull your paddle to wait for your friends, and don't hog a court game after game while others wait. The rotation is sacred because it's what keeps open play fair for everyone. Learn how your local court does it and follow it.

## 4. Be honest about your skill level

When you're sorting games or joining open play, tell the truth about where you're at. Sandbagging — claiming you're a beginner when you're clearly not, or vice versa — wrecks the balance and the fun for everyone. Nobody enjoys a lopsided game. Honest self-rating means better, closer matches, which is more fun for you too.

## 5. Don't walk behind a live court

If you need to get to the next court, wait until the point ends before crossing behind an active game, and never cut through the middle of play. Balls fly, players lunge, and a stray person crossing the baseline is both dangerous and a rally-killer. A quick "point?" and a two-second wait is all it takes.

## 6. Return stray balls safely

When a ball rolls onto your court from next door, stop your point, hold the ball, and roll or pass it back safely once play stops — don't smack it across the complex. And when it's your ball that's gone astray, call "ball on!" loudly so the neighbouring court knows to pause. Rogue balls under someone's feet are the number one cause of court injuries.

## 7. Keep the score calling clear

In doubles, the server calls the score loud and clear before every serve — all three numbers. It's not just a rule, it's a courtesy so nobody's confused mid-rally. If there's a genuine disagreement about the score, replay the point rather than arguing. It's a casual game; nobody's title is on the line.

## 8. Watch the trash talk and the celebrations

A bit of banter is part of the fun, and a good "nice shot!" to an opponent is always welcome. But reading the room matters. Over-the-top celebrations after a point, aggressive stares, or grinding an opponent into the ground in a friendly game all sour the mood. Compliment good shots — including your opponents' — and keep it light.

## 9. Mind your targets

You will, at some point, have a clean look at smashing the ball straight at an opponent. In competitive play, body shots are legal and part of the game. But in casual open play, especially with mixed levels, ease off. Blasting a beginner or an older player at close range isn't a flex, it's just unkind. Great players win with placement and patience, not by trying to dent someone.

## 10. Leave the court better than you found it

Pick up your ball cans, water bottles and tape. If you moved a net or borrowed equipment, put it back. Thank whoever organised the session. Public courts and clubs survive on goodwill, and the players who tidy up and say thanks are the ones who keep getting invited back. Small stuff, big reputation.

## Why this actually matters

Here's the thing about etiquette: it's not about being stiff or formal. Pickleball's entire appeal is that it's social, welcoming and fun. All these unwritten rules exist to protect exactly that. Follow them and you become the player people are glad to see walk up to the court. Ignore them and, well, word travels fast in a local scene.
