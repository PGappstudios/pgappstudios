Nobody prepares you for how physical a breakup feels. You brace yourself for sadness. You do not expect to feel like you are coming down with something.

If you are reading this a few days or a few weeks in, you are not broken and you are not overreacting. What you are feeling is common, it has a shape, and it does change. This is a plain description of that shape, and of the small things that tend to make the first weeks more survivable.

Before anything else: this article is general support, not medical care. If you are struggling badly, or if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor, a therapist, or a local crisis line today. That is not an overreaction either.

## Why the first weeks feel physically bad

### Sleep goes first

Most people lose sleep before they lose anything else. You fall asleep late because your mind will not stop replaying things, or you fall asleep fine and wake at four in the morning with your chest already tight. Then the tiredness makes the sadness heavier, and the heaviness makes sleep harder. It is a loop, and it is one of the reasons week two often feels worse than week one.

You do not need perfect sleep right now. You need enough of it to keep functioning. Aim for consistency rather than quality: same wake time every day, curtains open in the morning, phone somewhere you cannot reach from the bed.

### Appetite gets strange

Some people cannot eat. Others eat constantly and taste none of it. Both are normal responses to a nervous system that thinks something urgent is happening.

The practical fix is low effort and boring on purpose. Keep food in the house that requires no decision: bread, eggs, fruit, yoghurt, soup, something you can eat standing up. Skipping meals for days will make your mood worse in a way that has nothing to do with your ex and everything to do with blood sugar.

### The loop that will not stop

Rumination is the worst part for a lot of people. You replay the last conversation. You rewrite it. You build an argument you will never get to make. You imagine them happy without you, in detail, in high definition.

Your brain is not torturing you for fun. It is trying to solve a problem that no longer has a solution, and it keeps running the search because the search feels like doing something. Naming that helps a little. So does giving the loop a container: fifteen minutes with a notebook where you let it all out on paper, then closing the notebook and going outside.

> You are not going to think your way out of this in the first month. You are going to live your way out of it, slowly, in very ordinary days that do not feel like progress while you are having them.

## The no contact question

Almost everyone asks some version of this: do I have to cut them off completely?

No contact is not a punishment and it is not a strategy to get someone back. It is a way of letting a wound close. Every message, every profile check, every small update reopens it. That is genuinely how it works for most people: contact resets the clock.

What tends to be true:

* Contact keeps hope alive, and hope is what makes grief last longer.
* Checking their social media feels like information. It is not. It is a photograph of the version of themselves they want strangers to see.
* Muting and unfollowing is kinder to yourself than relying on willpower forty times a day.
* If you share children, a home, a business, or pets, full silence is not available. Aim for low contact instead: logistics only, short, polite, no history, no feelings.

You do not have to announce it. You do not owe anyone a speech about boundaries. You can simply stop replying to things that do not need a reply.

And if you break it, which many people do, that is not a failure that undoes everything. Start again the next morning. This is a habit, not an exam.

## Build the smallest possible routine

The instinct in week one is to overhaul your life. Join a gym, learn a language, become a different person by October. That almost never survives contact with a Tuesday.

What works better is a routine so small it feels almost insulting:

1. Get up at the same time every day, even when you have nowhere to be.
2. Get outside within an hour of waking, even for ten minutes.
3. Eat something before noon.
4. Move your body once, gently. A walk counts. A walk always counts.
5. Message one human being who knows you.
6. Lights off at a fixed time, phone in another room.

That is it. Six things, none of them impressive. The point is not self improvement. The point is that a day with a shape is much harder to fall through than a day without one.

Add one small thing each week if you want. Do not add three.

## What people try, and what tends to help more

| What people try | What tends to help more |
|:---|:---|
| Checking their profile once a day | Muting and unfollowing so the decision is already made |
| Waiting for a text that explains everything | Accepting that closure is something you build, not something you receive |
| Getting straight into dating to feel wanted | A few weeks of being alone on purpose before you decide anything |
| Drinking to get through the evenings | A walk at the time of day you usually feel worst |
| Telling the story to anyone who will listen | Telling it fully to two or three people who actually know you |
| Deciding you will never trust anyone again | Noticing that thought, and not acting on it while you are this raw |
| Big life changes in week one | Small repeated changes across three months |
| Waiting to feel better before doing anything | Doing the small thing first, and letting the feeling catch up later |

Nothing on the left column makes you a fool. Every one of them is a reasonable attempt to stop hurting. They just tend to cost more than they give.

## How long does this take

Honestly? Nobody can tell you, and anyone who gives you a fixed number is guessing.

What is more useful than a timeline is knowing the shape. Healing is not a slope. It is jagged. You will have a good week and think you are through it, then a song or a street or a smell will put you back on the floor, and you will feel like you lost all of it. You did not. The good week was real. The bad day was also real. Both are part of the same process.

Some markers people notice over time:

* You go a whole morning without thinking about them, and only notice in the afternoon.
* Their name comes up and your stomach does not drop.
* You remember the relationship accurately, including the parts that did not work.
* You stop imagining running into them.
* You start making plans that have nothing to do with them at all.

Those arrive at their own pace. Anniversaries, birthdays and holidays will still land hard for a while after that, and that is normal too.

## When to reach out for professional help

Grief after a breakup is not an illness. But it can sit on top of something that needs real care, and it is worth knowing where that line is.

Please speak to a doctor or a qualified mental health professional if:

* You are having thoughts of harming yourself, or you feel like you cannot keep yourself safe.
* You have barely slept or barely eaten for more than a week or two.
* You cannot work, study, or care for people who depend on you.
* You are drinking or using substances much more than usual to get through the day.
* You feel numb and flat for weeks rather than sad in waves.
* The relationship involved control, fear, threats, or violence.
* Weeks are passing and nothing at all is shifting.

Asking for help early is not weakness and it is not dramatic. It is the same logic as seeing someone about a cough that will not clear.

## One last thing

You are going to want this to be over. It will not be over on your schedule, and there is no clever shortcut hiding somewhere that you have failed to find.

What you can do is make the days slightly kinder to yourself. Sleep at roughly the same hours. Eat something. Walk. Talk to someone who knows you. Leave the phone alone. Repeat that on the days it feels pointless, because those are the ones that count.

It gets lighter. Not on a timetable, and not in a straight line, but it does.
